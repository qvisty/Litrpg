# Warcraft, the Technical Layer

The book takes its military hardware seriously, and a reader who comes for the technical war
history (armour, guns, aircraft, ballistics, doctrine, and how all of it evolved across the
war) should find real meat here. This document is the author's reference for getting it right
and for binding it to the system. House style applies: no em-dashes, no semicolons.

> **The core idea.** A penetration table and the system's ledger are the same cold arithmetic.
> Both reduce living men and machines to numbers and odds. The technical layer is not decoration.
> It rhymes with the book's spine, the abattoir that keeps accounts. We lean on that parallel.

---

## Principles

1. **Accurate to the month and the front.** Materiel, markings, doctrine, and what was actually
   available where and when. When a fact is load-bearing and uncertain, verify it before the
   chapter ships and note the source in the war anchors table.
2. **Dark, never gun-porn.** Technical detail earns its place through tension, dread, and
   consequence, never through a catalogue. The medic sees what a round does to a body. The crew
   feels the round that did not penetrate as a bell struck on the inside of their own skull. We
   do not glorify the machine. We respect it and we fear it.
3. **Through an expert eye.** Technical detail rides on a character who would genuinely know it,
   in his own idiom. A sniper thinks in mils and wind and drop. A tank commander thinks in
   ranges and angles and where the enemy's armour is thin. The reader learns by standing behind
   a competent, frightened man, not by reading a manual.
4. **Load-bearing only.** If a detail does not change a decision, the tension, or the meaning,
   it is cut. Same rule as the stat readouts.
5. **It evolves.** The hardware escalates across the war, and the players witness it. That
   escalation mirrors the system's own (the interface maturing, the scores climbing, the harvest
   nearing). Machines get better at killing as the thing gets better at reaping.

## How the technical layer couples to the system

This is the part the author asked for, the binding of the war tech to the player stats.

### Skills are technical mastery
The system recognises and ranks real competence, and we render that competence with real
specifics. Candidate technical skills (named the moment the system first marks them):

- **Marksmanship / Ballistics** (Mercer): range estimation, wind, drop, the cold reading of a
  scope, the patience of the hide.
- **Gunnery / Armour** (a tank player, if we add one, or observed by others): where a given gun
  defeats a given plate at a given range and angle, where it never will, the shot trap, the
  flank, the track.
- **Demolitions** (Aksel): charges, cutting order, fusing, the physics of bringing a thing down.
- **Field Medicine** (Jonas): wound ballistics from the other side, what a high-velocity round,
  a fragment, or a blast does to tissue, and the brutal triage math of who can be saved.
- **Aircraft recognition and the fear of the Jabo:** knowing the sound and silhouette that means
  you have seconds to leave the road.

### Penetration, the hit, and survival are the throw
Armour penetration was genuinely probabilistic, so it maps onto our d20 throw (see `SYSTEM.md`)
without strain. At the decisive instant, the historical factors set the difficulty and the throw
resolves it, with favour bending the result.

- The real inputs are the real inputs: gun and round, range, the target's armour thickness and
  slope, the angle of impact, plate quality, and the failure modes (a ricochet off slope, an
  APCBC round shattering on face-hardened plate, a HEAT round's chemical jet that does not care
  about range but needed a hit it rarely got).
- **Fumble:** the round shatters, bounces, the weapon jams (the Sten was infamous for it), the
  fuse fails. War's machinery turning on its user.
- **Triumph:** the impossible shot, the weak point found, the shot trap under the mantlet, the
  88 that finds a Sherman at a mile.
- **Favour bends it.** High favour is the crew that walks away from the brewed-up tank, the round
  that deflects when it should have killed. They never know they were kept.

### The interface speaks in fire-control
As the interface matures, it surfaces the cold technical assessment before the throw, range, gun,
odds of penetration, like a fire-control solution only the player can see. It usually delivers
bad news (frontal penetration unlikely, go for the flank, you will not make this in time), which
deepens dread rather than granting power. The same ledger that prices a man's soul also prices a
plate of rolled homogeneous armour, and that sameness is the point.

## Materiel timeline (anchors to keep chapters correct)

A working, high-level guide so chapters stay period-true. Fill the war anchors table in the
story bible with specifics as they are used, and verify exact figures at drafting time.

### 1943, occupied Denmark (Part One)
Small arms and sabotage, not armour. The resistance lives on what Britain air-drops and what it
improvises. Sten submachine guns (cheap, 9mm, jam-prone, a natural home for a fumble), pistols,
British plastic explosive, time pencils and fuses, the German garrison's rifles and the menace
of the occupier's pistols and cells. The technical texture here is the craft of sabotage and the
intimate hardware of a hunted city.

### 1944, Normandy and north-west Europe (Part Two onward)
The armour war and the air war, where the technical layer comes into its own.

- **German armour:** the Panzer IV, the war's workhorse, by now the long 75mm gun (KwK 40) and
  bolt-on side skirts, a useful illustration of evolution from the stubby infantry-support tank
  of 1939. The Panther, superb sloped frontal armour and a lethal high-velocity 75mm (KwK 42),
  but with weaker flanks and an early mantlet shot trap that could deflect a round down through
  the hull roof. The Tiger I, flat 100mm frontal plate and the dreaded 88mm KwK 36, fearsome
  head-on and beatable from the flank. The 8.8cm Flak in the anti-tank role, the gun the Allies
  feared most.
- **Allied armour:** the Sherman, reliable and numerous, its 75mm M3 gun excellent against
  infantry but unable to defeat a Panther's glacis at fighting range, hence the habit of aiming
  for tracks, flanks, and optics, and hence the dread nickname the crews earned. The Sherman
  Firefly with the British 17-pounder, one of the few Allied tank guns that could kill a Panther
  or Tiger frontally at combat range, prized and singled out by the enemy. The US 76mm, better
  than the 75 but still short against the Panther glacis.
- **Anti-tank in infantry hands:** the bazooka and the German Panzerfaust and Panzerschreck,
  shaped-charge weapons whose penetration did not fall off with range the way kinetic rounds did,
  but which demanded nerve and closeness. The British PIAT, a spring-and-spigot oddity, no back
  blast, vicious recoil, short range.
- **The air war:** Allied air supremacy made German daylight movement deadly. The rocket-firing
  Hawker Typhoon and the P-47, the Jabos, the reason a column moved at night and a man listened
  to the sky. The psychological weight of owning the air, or of losing it.

### Later war (if the story reaches it)
The escalation continues, the Tiger II, heavier guns, the thinning of German skies and fuel, the
grinding industrial arithmetic that the system's own accountancy quietly echoes.

## Tone guardrails

- Never let the hardware crowd out the human. A tank is a crew of frightened men in a steel box
  that can become an oven in seconds. Keep the body in the machine.
- Bad news more often than good. The technical truth of this war is mostly that better machines
  killed you first.
- The cold math is a theme, not a flex. Every penetration table we evoke should, somewhere,
  remind the reader of the other ledger.
