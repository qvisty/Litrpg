# Chapter Two, Editor's Edition (temporary)

Full text of Chapter Two with editorial commentary interleaved. The **EDITOR** blocks are
scaffolding and will be deleted. The clean chapter lives at `src/part-1/chapter-02.md`. House
style applies, no em-dashes, no semicolons.

> **EDITOR, what this chapter is for.** Chapter One withheld the system and ended on first
> contact. Chapter Two has one main job, to deliver the genre pleasure the author asked for,
> a player who knows he has stats and engages with them, while quietly turning that pleasure
> into dread. Aksel learns to read his own ledger, takes pride in it, and builds the very
> theory that will damn him, all without understanding it. Second job, plant favour as a felt,
> wrong thing during an action beat, so the harvest pays off later. Third, put a living person
> (Margit) in the room so the thing being harvested has a face.

---

Margit found him on the roof at first light, which was where he went now when he could not make
the letters stop.

> **EDITOR.** We open after a time skip (three nights) on a liminal image, a roof at dawn, a man
> who cannot sleep. It signals at once that first contact changed him. "Make the letters stop"
> tells the reader the system is now a permanent, unwanted tenant in his head, the opposite of a
> power fantasy.

He had not slept. Since the shop and the man in the good coat, three nights ago, he had learned a
thing about the weather behind his eyes that frightened him more than the Gestapo did, which was
that he could call it.

> **EDITOR, the mechanic maturing on the page.** This is the interface climbing from Stage 0 to
> Stage 1. In Chapter One it surfaced on its own and he was a passenger. Now he can summon it.
> Notice the framing, it "frightened him more than the Gestapo did." The genre beat (you can open
> your sheet now) is delivered as a horror beat. That is the tone we want every time a new
> function unlocks.

They had names now ... He had watched all four climb, slowly, for years he now understood, and he
could not make himself be ashamed of being good at the work. A man should be good at saving people.

> **EDITOR, the trap dressed as pride.** Here is the player engaging with his stats exactly as a
> LitRPG reader enjoys, naming them, tracking growth, taking pride. And the pride is the hook.
> "A man should be good at saving people" is decent and true and it is also the rationalisation
> that lets him keep feeding the thing. We let him be right and doomed in the same sentence.

It was the fifth that he sat on the roof about. ... The theory was that the four were what the
thing gave him, and the fifth was what it took, and that it had been very careful, for two years,
to make the taking feel like a gift.

> **EDITOR, the thesis, spoken by a man who does not know he is right.** This is the cost
> (Distance) rendered as the character's own private theory. He has worked out the literal truth
> of the system, gift versus taking, without any idea what it means or where it leads. Dramatic
> irony at full power. The reader who reaches Layer 6 will remember this roof. I keep the word
> DISTANCE in the cold interface voice and let Aksel translate it into his own human image, a
> tide that only comes in, so the mechanic and the feeling are the same thing.

"You will catch your death up here," Margit said ... She had lit his cigarette in the dark in the
first winter when his hands shook.

> **EDITOR, the human anchor.** Margit steps off the page she was named on in Chapter One and
> becomes a body next to his. The cigarette callback does the work of a whole backstory in a
> clause. Strategically she matters because the system feeds on attachment and mercy, so every
> person Aksel loves raises his value and his danger. She is, in ledger terms, fuel. We never say
> that. We just make the reader love her too, which is the same trap he is in.

I should tell her. ... and then he did the thing he always did, which was to carry it instead.

> **EDITOR.** His isolation is the engine of the next chapters. The secret cannot be shared, so
> the dread has nowhere to go. "Carry it instead" reaches back to his Chapter One creed about a
> man being the sum of what he refuses to put down. We are building a pattern so that when the
> harvest comes for exactly the things he carries, it reads as inevitable, not arbitrary.

the cold word rose unbidden at the edge of his sight, as though it had heard him lie and wished to
keep the record straight

> **EDITOR, the system as an active character.** The author asked for a system that acts unbidden,
> not just when summoned. Here it interrupts a tender lie to mark it. It is petty, exact, and
> always listening, which is far more frightening than a loud system would be.

The run to the coast was the kind that should not have worked. ... papers that were good enough for
a bored man and not good enough for a frightened one.

> **EDITOR, grounding the speculative in real tradecraft.** Before any system magic, the rescue is
> built from period detail, the false floor, the paregoric to quiet the children, the forged
> papers. This buys the reader's trust so that when the interface speaks a paragraph later, it
> lands inside a real world rather than a game.

> A checkpoint stands beyond the next rise. New tonight.
> They will look in the back.
> You have perhaps forty seconds to decide who you are.

> **EDITOR, the in-scene channel, load-bearing.** This is the system woven into action, not an
> end-of-chapter readout. It does not fight for him, it gives him information and a clock, and he
> still has to be the one in the ditch. The line "decide who you are" is the system's flat idiom
> and also the book's whole question. Note there is no d20 throw here. The throw is being saved
> for the night he dies, so the first time the reader feels dice is the night they come up against
> him.

a young German in a wet cape looked down the road at the shape of a peasant having a piss in a ditch
and felt, Aksel would think later, nothing at all. Nothing pointed him down the road.

> **EDITOR, favour planted as wrongness.** This is the seed of the whole harvest mechanic. The
> escape is too clean. The enemy simply fails to do the lethal, easy thing, for no reason. We
> route it through the German feeling "nothing," which both keeps the enemy human and plants the
> idea that something arranged this. On a reread, this is favour spending itself to keep its
> investment alive. Right now it just feels like luck, which is exactly what favour is supposed to
> feel like.

> Cleanly done.
> Favour: with him.
> ... It had the shape of a thing being said about him by someone he could not see, in a room he
> was not allowed into.

> **EDITOR, the kindest lie named.** The reader meets the word favour in the prose for the first
> time, and Aksel's unease does the foreshadowing for us. He does not like being favoured and
> cannot say why. That instinct is correct and he will never get to find out why.

It rewarded the saving of children. What rewards the saving of children is good. ... and the
terrible ease of his own hands.

> **EDITOR, the benevolence lie at full strength, with the cost showing underneath.** He restates
> the comforting syllogism from Chapter One, now load-tested by a real rescue, so it is more
> convincing and more wrong. And directly beneath it I let the cost surface, "the terrible ease of
> his own hands," so the reader can feel the undertow even as Aksel refuses to look at it.

"We did a good thing tonight," Margit said softly ... "Yes," Aksel said, and waited to feel it, and
felt the word rise instead.
> DISTANCE: rising.

> **EDITOR, the chapter's last turn of the screw.** He does a good thing, says so, and reaches for
> the feeling, and the feeling is gone, replaced by the readout of its absence. This is the
> tragedy in miniature, every act of goodness now buys a little more numbness. Ending on the bare
> interface line lets the cost have the last word.

> word neither Aksel nor the reader can yet read correctly.
