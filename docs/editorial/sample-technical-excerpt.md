# Sample, the Technical Layer in Action (demo, not canon)

A standalone demonstration excerpt, set later in the book (Normandy, summer 1944), to show how
the technical war detail and the d20 throw read on the page once the interface has matured enough
to speak. The POV here is a stand-in Sherman commander, Sergeant Doyle, not one of the three
protagonists. Style is the locked house style: no em-dashes, no semicolons, British spelling,
dark and grounded. This is a feel test, easy to discard or adapt.

---

The lane was sunk between two hedgebanks, and the Panther sat at the end of it like a problem
with no answer.

Doyle saw it before the system did, which was how he knew it was real and not nerves. Eight
hundred yards, hull down behind a fold of pasture, only the long gun and the front plate showing,
that great sloped slab of glacis the colour of a wet road. He knew the angle of it the way he
knew the back of Nora's hand. Fifty-five degrees off the vertical, eighty millimetres thick, and
behind it a gun that would open his tank like a tin of pilchards before he had finished the
sentence he was about to say.

He said it anyway. "Gunner, traverse left, on."

Then the cold came in over the top of his own thoughts, the way it had since the spring, and laid
its arithmetic across the hedge in pale figures only he could read.

> RANGE 760. TARGET, frontal.
> 75mm M61 against 80mm at 55 degrees.
> Penetration: no.
> It will not go in. It has never gone in. Choose again.

"Bastard thing," Doyle breathed, and he did not mean the system, and he did mean the system. It
told him nothing his bones did not already know. The seventy-five would star the Panther's glacis
and skip off into the orchard like a flung stone, and the German would take his time, because a
man with a gun like that could afford to, and then there would be the bell-note that a crew heard
once and never again.

"Driver, reverse, get the bank between us." The Sherman lurched. Soil and torn hawthorn came down
the front, and for a moment the hedgebank was a wall of England between Doyle and the thing that
wanted him. He keyed the troop net. "Sunray, this is Three. Panther, sunken lane, hull down,
I can't touch him from the front. Where is Michael." Michael was the Firefly, the one tank in the
troop with the seventeen-pounder, the one gun in a mile that could put a round through that plate
and mean it, and the Germans knew it too, which was why they shot the Fireflies first and why
Michael's commander had taken to lashing a length of drainpipe to his barrel to make it look like
an ordinary tank from the air.

"Three, Sunray. Michael's coming up your right. Hold him. Buy a minute."

A minute. Doyle looked at the hedgebank and the grey light and thought, quite calmly, that a
minute was a great deal of money in this country and he was not sure he had it in his pocket.

"Up and over the bank on my word," he told the driver. "We show him our side for as long as it
takes to be wrong about us, then we are gone. Gunner, the instant we crest, his running gear, the
front road wheels, you will not kill him but you will lame him and a lame Panther in a lane is a
dead one when Michael comes. Understand me."

"Sergeant."

"Now."

The Sherman climbed the bank, nose to the sky, and came down with a crash that threw Doyle against
the hatch rim, and the world filled with the flank of the German tank, the interleaved wheels, the
worn black cross, a loader's white face turning in the side vision block. And the cold came down on
all of it at once, fast now, the way it came when everything was about to be decided.

> TARGET presents flank. 50mm at 0 degrees, 740.
> Penetration: probable.
> THE THROW.

There was the held breath, the second that was not a second, the whole green lane gone thick as
glass. Doyle had learned not to pray in it. There was nothing in there to pray to, only the thing
that kept the count, and it did not take requests.

The German fired first.

He should not have missed. At that range, on that beam, the long seventy-five should have gone in
under Doyle's feet and turned the fighting compartment into a single white noise. Doyle felt the
strike as a flat enormous slap on the air and a line of fire drawn down the side of the turret,
and the round went away across the pasture still whole, deflected off the rounded edge of the
mantlet at an angle no gunner chooses and no one can refuse, and a man in the troop would say
afterwards that he had never seen the like, that Doyle's tank had been dead and had simply
declined the appointment.

> Against you. Withheld.
> Favour: with him.

Doyle did not read it. He never read it. "Fire," he said, and the gunner fired.

The seventy-five was no good against the front of a Panther and it was a different animal against
the side at seven hundred yards. The round took the German between the second and third road
wheels, and the throw came up the way it almost never did, the high impossible end of it, and
something behind the armour that was not meant to be touched was touched. The Panther did not
explode. It was worse than that, and slower. It stopped, and a thin grey smoke came up out of the
engine deck, polite at first, and then the hatches came open and the men began to climb out, and
the men were the part Doyle never got used to, the part the system never costed for him, four
young men and then a fifth coming out of a steel box that was filling with fire, one of them
already burning along the arm and beating at it as he ran.

> ASSESSED. Retained.

"Cease fire," Doyle said, though no one had asked to go on. He watched the crew make the far hedge
and he did not let the gunner traverse onto them, and he told himself, the way he told himself
most days now, that the not-firing was courage, and he noticed, distantly, that his hands on the
hatch rim were perfectly steady, and had been for a long time.
