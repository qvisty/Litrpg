# Chapter One

The cold came up off the harbour and found every gap in Aksel's coat, and he stood in it
without moving, because moving was how you got seen.

Across Sankt Annæ Plads the German staff car had been idling for eleven minutes. He knew it was
eleven because he had counted, the way Margit had taught him, one thousand and one, one thousand
and two, a count to keep the fear from climbing into the hands. Fear in the hands was how a man
dropped a parcel, or fumbled a door, or shot when he should have walked. Aksel did not intend to
do any of those things tonight. He had a satchel against his hip with four pounds of plastic
explosive in it, moulded to look like a wheel of cheese under greaseproof paper, and a route home
that crossed no bridge twice.

The driver got out to stamp his feet. Young. Bored. A boy doing a cold job in a country that had
not yet decided to hate him properly.

*Walk,* Aksel told himself, and walked.

This was the part Margit could not teach, the part that was only his. As he came off the square
and into the mouth of Kvæsthusgade, with the boy soldier nine steps behind him and closing, the
thing that lived behind his eyes opened them.

It was not a voice. He had tried for two years to find the word for it and the closest he had got
was *weather*. A pressure that arrived before he understood why, a tilt in the world that told
him which way the ground wanted him to fall. Tonight it told him the boy was not following him. The
boy was only cold and walking the same way a cold man walks, toward light, toward the tram. The
weather in Aksel's chest went from iron to ordinary, and a line of pale letters surfaced at the
edge of his sight, where no one else could read them, where nothing was written.

> NERVE held under observation.
> ASSESSED. Retained.

He had stopped asking what it meant. In the beginning he had thought he was going mad, and had
gone to confession over it, and the old priest had told him that God speaks to soldiers in the
language they can bear, which was a kind answer and a useless one. Then in the spring of the first
year the letters had told him a thing he could not have known, that the man he was about to trust
with the Helsingør crossing had already sold three others to the Gestapo, and Aksel had walked
away from the meeting on the strength of nothing but pale letters, and four nights later they took
the man who went in his place. After that he stopped calling it madness. He did not know what to
call it instead. He called it nothing. He used it.

The boy soldier turned off toward the tram. Aksel went the other way, into the dark, and let his
breathing out slow.

He made the drop in the cellar of the bicycle shop on Borgergade, where Holger was waiting with
the wireless and a face like a closed door.

"You are early," Holger said, which from Holger was warmth.

"The car sat too long. I went round." Aksel set the satchel on the workbench with the care a man
gives a sleeping child, and only then noticed his own hands. Steady. Always steady now. There had
been a year, early, when they had shaken so badly after a job that he could not light his own
cigarette, and Margit had lit it for him in the dark and said nothing, and he had loved her for
the nothing. He could not remember the last time they shook. He told himself that was courage. He
told himself a man got used to things. The letters surfaced again, briefly, as he lifted his hands
into the lamplight to look at them, as though the looking itself were an act it wished to mark.

> ENDURANCE recognised.
> Distance: rising.

"What does that mean," he said, before he could stop himself.

Holger looked up. "What does what mean."

"Nothing." Aksel folded the greaseproof paper along its old creases. "Talking to myself. It is
the cold."

Holger grunted and went back to the wireless, and the moment closed over like water, and Aksel let
it. There were things you did not say even in a cellar, even to a man who would die before he gave
you up. Aksel had decided long ago that the weather behind his eyes was the most dangerous secret
he carried, more than the names, more than the drops. A man with a secret army could be shot. A man
who heard letters that were not there would be sent somewhere quiet and kind and useless, and the
war would go on without him, and he could not allow that. He was good at this. He was, he knew with
a flat private certainty that he never spoke aloud, better at this than anyone he had ever met. The
letters seemed to think so too. They came more often now, and warmer, the way a teacher warms to a
pupil who is going to do the family proud.

He did not yet understand that the warmth was the danger. He thought, when he thought about it at
all, that a thing which praised a man for nerve and mercy and the saving of others must be on the
side of the saved. It rewarded the good in him. What rewards the good, he reasoned, must be good.
He was a careful man, and a brave one, and on this one thing he was a child holding out his hand to
the dark because the dark had always, so far, only given him sweets.

Upstairs the shop bell rang.

Both men went still. Holger's hand drifted to the drawer where the pistol was. Aksel lifted two
fingers, *wait,* and reached for the weather, and found it calm. Not iron. Not even grey.

"Customer," he murmured. "Stay."

He went up alone, wiping his hands on a rag, the shopkeeper's son and nothing more, and the man in
the doorway was already smiling. Older, neat, a good coat, the kind of Copenhagen face that could
be a schoolteacher or a tram inspector or a man who informed for money, and the weather said
nothing about him at all, which had never once happened before. Where every other soul Aksel had
ever stood near gave off some reading, some tilt, this man gave off the flat nothing of a wall, or
a window, or a thing that was not quite there.

"Forgive the hour," the man said, in Danish soft as a native's and wrong somehow in a way Aksel
could not name. "I am looking for a bicycle for my niece. Something that will last her a long
time." His eyes moved once around the dim shop, unhurried, and came back to Aksel, and his smile
did not reach them, and he said, as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world, "You have
done very well, you know. They will be pleased with you."

"Who will," said Aksel.

"Everyone," the man said warmly, "who is keeping count."

The cold came up off the harbour and found the gap in the door, and for the first time in a long
time, standing in the safe yellow lamplight of a shop he had bled to protect, Aksel Holt felt his
hands begin, very slightly, to shake.
