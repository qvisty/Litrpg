# Chapter One

The cold came up off the harbour and found every gap in Aksel's coat, and he stood in it
without moving, because moving was how you got seen.

Across Sankt Annæ Plads the German staff car had been idling for eleven minutes. He knew it was
eleven because he had counted, the way Margit had taught him in the first month, one thousand and
one, one thousand and two, a count to keep the fear from climbing into the hands. Fear in the
hands was how a man dropped a parcel, or fumbled a door, or fired when he should have walked.
Aksel did not intend to do any of those things tonight. He had a satchel against his hip with
four pounds of plastic explosive in it, moulded to look like a wheel of cheese under greaseproof
paper, and a route home that crossed no bridge twice.

The driver got out to stamp his feet. Young. Bored. A boy doing a cold job in a country that had
not yet decided to hate him properly.

*Walk,* Aksel told himself, and walked.

He came off the square and into the mouth of Kvæsthusgade with the boy soldier nine steps behind
him and closing, and he did not hurry, because hurrying was a kind of confession. He let his
shoulders go heavy, a tired man going home, and listened to the boots on the cobbles behind him.
Two years of this had taught him to hear the difference between a man who was following and a man
who was only walking the same cold street toward the same warm tram. These boots wanted the
tram. They turned off at the corner, toward the light, and Aksel let his breath out slow and did
not look back.

He made the drop in the cellar of the bicycle shop on Borgergade, where Holger waited with the
wireless and a face like a closed door.

"You are early," Holger said, which from Holger was warmth.

"The car sat too long. I went round." Aksel set the satchel on the workbench with the care a man
gives a sleeping child, and noticed, setting it down, that his hands were steady. They had not
always been. There had been a winter, the first one, when they shook so badly after a job that he
could not light his own cigarette, and Margit had lit it for him in the dark and said nothing,
and he had loved her for the nothing. Somewhere along the way the shaking had stopped. He told
himself that was courage. He told himself a man got used to anything, even this, and on the good
nights he believed it.

Holger uncapped the wireless and began the slow business of the valves warming. "Two more
families went over from Dragør last night," he said, not looking up. "The doctor and his people.
A herring boat took them. They are in Malmö by now, eating someone else's bread."

"Good," Aksel said, and meant it more than he had meant anything in weeks. He had carried an old
woman down to the water at Dragør himself, eight nights ago, her whole life in a pillowcase, her
hand cold as a fish in his, and had stood in the shallows with the sea filling his boots until
the boat was only a darker piece of the dark. He did not tell Holger that part. You did not say
the parts that mattered, not even in a cellar, not even to a man who would die before he gave you
up. But he carried them. He had decided, somewhere in this long grey war, that carrying them was
the whole of the point, that a man was only the sum of what he refused to put down.

"You are too soft for this work," Holger said, without heat, the way you tell a man the truth you
admire him for.

"Probably," Aksel said, and smiled, and upstairs the shop bell rang.

Both men went still. Holger's hand drifted to the drawer where the pistol was. Aksel lifted two
fingers, *wait,* and listened, and heard nothing that frightened him.

"Customer," he murmured. "Stay."

He went up alone, wiping his hands on a rag, the shopkeeper's son and nothing more. The man in
the doorway was already smiling. Older, neat, a good coat, the kind of Copenhagen face that could
be a schoolteacher or a tram inspector or a man who informed for money. There was something not
quite right about him and Aksel could not have said what. A stillness, perhaps. The cold had come
in with everyone else all evening, on their collars and their breath, and it had not come in with
this man at all.

"Forgive the hour," the man said, in Danish soft as a native's and wrong somehow in a way Aksel
could not name. "I am looking for a bicycle for my niece. Something that will last her a long
time." His eyes moved once around the dim shop, unhurried, and came back to Aksel. "You have done
very well, you know," he said, as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world. "They are
pleased with you."

"Who is," said Aksel.

"Everyone," the man said warmly, "who is keeping count."

And the world tilted.

It came without sound and without pain, a pressure behind the eyes, and then, where in all his
life there had never been anything, there were words. Pale letters lay over the dim shop like
frost on the inside of a window, and they were about him. His own name was among them, and beside
it a column of things he had never spoken to a living soul, the steadiness, the quickness, the
nights at the water, set down in a cold even hand as though he were stock in a ledger. He
understood only one thing before it was gone, and the one thing was this. That something had been
watching him. All of it. The cellars and the cobbles and the winter his hands shook and the old
woman cold as a fish, watching and writing him down, and that not once, in two years, had he felt
the weight of its eyes.

Then it was gone, and the shop was only a shop. The bell was still swinging. The man in the good
coat was already out in the street, his back turning, and he did not look like a man who had said
anything strange at all.

Aksel stood in the safe yellow lamplight of a place he had bled to protect, and for the first
time in a long time, his hands began, very slightly, to shake.
