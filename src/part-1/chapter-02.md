# Chapter Two

Margit found him on the roof at first light, which was where he went now when he could not make
the letters stop.

He had not slept. Since the shop and the man in the good coat, three nights ago, he had learned a
thing about the weather behind his eyes that frightened him more than the Gestapo did, which was
that he could call it. If he sat very still and wanted to, the way you want a sneeze to finish, the
pale letters rose of their own accord and let themselves be read. He had spent three nights reading
them the way a man reads a letter from a doctor, again and again, looking for the word that was not
there.

They had names now. They had always had names, he supposed, but in the shop they had been a blur,
and now they held still long enough to be counted. Four of them he did not mind. The steadiness.
The quickness of seeing. The deep thing that kept him on his feet. The one that made men do what he
asked. He had watched all four climb, slowly, for years he now understood, and he could not make
himself be ashamed of being good at the work. A man should be good at saving people.

It was the fifth that he sat on the roof about.

> DISTANCE.

That was all it ever said of it, the one word, and a sense of a tide that only came in. It had
surfaced a month before the shop and it had not stopped, and unlike the others it gave him nothing
back when it rose, no steadiness, no edge, only the word and the cold. He had a theory now, sitting
in the wet grey light over the rooftops of a city that belonged to other men. The theory was that
the four were what the thing gave him, and the fifth was what it took, and that it had been very
careful, for two years, to make the taking feel like a gift.

"You will catch your death up here," Margit said, and sat down beside him on the cold lead, and put
her shoulder against his, and he came back into his body a little.

She was not beautiful and he had stopped, a long time ago, being able to see her any way but as the
best thing the war had left him. She had taught him to count against the fear. She had lit his
cigarette in the dark in the first winter when his hands shook. He thought, looking at her, that he
should tell her. He thought it the way he had thought it every day for three days, and then he did
the thing he always did, which was to carry it instead.

"There is a boat tonight," she said. "Out of Dragor. The Arnsteins, and the old cantor, and two
children who are not theirs but are theirs now. I need a second man to the water."

"You have me," Aksel said.

She looked at him for a moment longer than she needed to. "You are somewhere else lately."

"I am right here," he said, and it was almost true, and the cold word rose unbidden at the edge of
his sight, as though it had heard him lie and wished to keep the record straight, and then it sank
again, and he made his face be a face she would not worry about.

The run to the coast was the kind that should not have worked.

They went down in a fish lorry with the cantor folded into a false floor that stank of herring and
diesel, and the children dosed with a little paregoric so they would not cry, and Margit up front
with papers that were good enough for a bored man and not good enough for a frightened one. Aksel
rode in the back with his hand flat on the false floor as if he could keep the old man breathing by
touch, and somewhere south of the city, on a straight black road through flat fields, the weather
went to iron.

He did not know how he knew. That was the part he had stopped pretending to understand. He only knew
the tilt of it, the way the night leaned, and then the letters came fast and without being asked.

> A checkpoint stands beyond the next rise. New tonight.
> They will look in the back.
> You have perhaps forty seconds to decide who you are.

He had no memory, afterward, of choosing. He banged twice on the cab, the signal for slow, and was
over the tailgate and walking back along the verge before the lorry had properly stopped, a farm
labourer caught short, fumbling at his flies in the ditch with his back to the road, the most boring
and most human thing a man could be doing on a cold road at night. The motorcycle came over the rise
with its hooded lamp and its second man in the sidecar, and it slowed at the lorry, and a torch went
over Margit's papers, and a young German in a wet cape looked down the road at the shape of a peasant
having a piss in a ditch and felt, Aksel would think later, nothing at all. Nothing pointed him down
the road. Nothing made him walk the forty steps to the back of the lorry and lift the false floor and
end all of them. He simply waved it on, because waving it on was easier than not, and the lorry ground
away toward the coast with everyone in it still alive.

Aksel stood in the ditch a moment longer than he had to, doing up his trousers with hands that were
perfectly steady, and the letters surfaced, quiet now, almost kind.

> Cleanly done.
> Favour: with him.

He did not know what the second line meant and he did not like it. It had the shape of a thing being
said about him by someone he could not see, in a room he was not allowed into. But the boat took the
Arnsteins and the cantor and the two borrowed children off the dark shingle two hours later, and
Aksel stood in the freezing shallows again with the sea filling his boots, and watched another small
piece of the dark detach itself and carry eight people toward a country that would let them live, and
he decided, the way a tired man decides, that a thing which arranged such nights could not be his
enemy. It rewarded the saving of children. What rewards the saving of children is good. He held the
thought the way he held all the others, close, and did not look at it too hard, because if he looked
too hard he could feel, underneath it, the cold tide coming in, and the terrible ease of his own
hands.

On the way back, empty, Margit drove and he watched the fields go by and felt the thing he had begun
to feel after every job now, which was almost nothing. A clean grey calm where the shaking used to
be. He told himself it was exhaustion. He told himself a man got used to anything.

"We did a good thing tonight," Margit said softly, watching the road.

"Yes," Aksel said, and waited to feel it, and felt the word rise instead.

> DISTANCE: rising.
