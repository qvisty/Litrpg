# Chapter Three

The boy on the table was nineteen and would keep the leg, and Jonas Keller counted that,
privately, against the great ledger of the world, as a man might put a single coin against a debt
he knew he would never finish paying.

The aid post had been a dance hall once. There were still gilt sconces on the walls above the cots,
and a mirrored ball somewhere up in the dark of the ceiling that caught the lamplight now and then
and threw a turning fleck of it across the wounded, as though the room remembered being happy. Jonas
worked under it with his sleeves rolled and his hands sure, and the boy with the leg watched his face
the way the wounded always did, reading it for the verdict, and Jonas gave him the face he kept for
exactly this, the calm one, the one that said you are not going to die tonight, not on my table, not
while I have anything to say about it.

He had wanted to be a doctor. He had been three years toward it in Freiburg when the letters came,
not the pale ones, the ordinary murderous ones with the eagle on them, and now he was a Sanitäter in
a stolen dance hall in a city that hated him politely, and he was, God help him, good at it. He sewed
the boy's leg and set the drain and did not let his hands hurry, and when it was done and the morphia
had the boy somewhere kinder, the cold writing rose at the edge of Jonas's sight the way it had for
two years, uninvited, exact.

> A life kept that the night meant to take.
> Assessed. This is recognised in you.

"I know," Jonas murmured, to no one the other men could see. "You always tell me."

He had stopped being frightened of it a long time ago and had started, which was worse, to need it.
In a war that asked him every day to be part of a great machine for the ending of men, the cold thing
behind his eyes kept a separate account, and on that account he was not a number on a field-post
roster, he was the sum of the boys who still had their legs. He clung to it the way the drowning cling
to the thing that is also dragging them down. He had noticed, the way you notice a tooth going bad,
that the more he leaned on it the less he felt at the cot-side, that the grief that should have flooded
him at the ones he lost now came from somewhere further off each month, a sea heard through a wall. He
had words of his own for the four that sharpened him. For the fifth thing there was only the ledger's
word. HOLLOW, it said, and beside the word it kept a figure, the way a pharmacist keeps the level in a
jar, and the figure never went down. He tried not to look at it, because a healer who cannot feel is
just a clean pair of hands, and a clean pair of hands will do anything it is told.

The trucks came at the end of the week, in the rain, after the curfew bell.

He was not meant to be in the street and he was in the street, walking back from the requisitioned
pharmacy with a satchel of sulfa against his chest, when the lorries came down past the synagogue with
their canvas backs and their escort of men who were not soldiers, men in the black of a different and
worse service, and Jonas stood in a doorway with the rain coming off the lintel and watched them take
a city's worth of people out of their beds. He saw an old man carried out still holding his hat. He
saw a woman counting children on a step, the way you count them at a railway platform, her lips
moving, one, two, three, and stop, and begin again, because the number would not stay still. He saw a
boy of about nine look directly at him, at the uniform, at the eagle on his chest, with no hatred in
it at all, only the flat enormous question of a child who has been woken into something with no shape,
and Jonas Keller, who could keep a life on a table that the night meant to take, stood in a doorway
with a satchel of medicine and kept nothing, did nothing, was nothing but a grey shape the rain ran
off, and the cold writing did not rise to comfort him, and he understood for the first time that it
never would for this. There was no account on which standing in a doorway was a life kept. The ledger
was silent the way a courtroom is silent. He had thought the thing rewarded mercy. He learned, in the
doorway, that it only rewarded the mercy you actually did, and that for the mercy you swallowed it had
no entry at all, and somehow that was the most honest and most terrible thing it had ever shown him.

He went back to the dance hall and was sick in the latrine and then washed his hands for a long time,
which was the only prayer he had left.

It was very late, and the rain had thinned, and he was sitting on the back step with a cigarette he
was not smoking when it happened, the new thing, the thing that had never happened in two years.

It came as a warmth, off across the dark roofs of the city, faint as a candle seen through fog and
just as certain. A direction. A sense, without a single word to carry it, of something out there in
the wet night that was the same as the thing behind his own eyes. Another account being kept.
Somewhere over there, north and east, toward the water, a man he would never meet was being written
down by the same cold hand that wrote him, and for one moment, across a city that wanted them to kill
each other, Jonas Keller felt the unbearable and ridiculous comfort of not being the only one.

Then his own letters surfaced, quiet, as if they had felt him notice, and told him a thing they had
never told him before, and would not explain.

> You are not the only ledger in this city.
> Do not go looking. It is not a kindness, what finds the others.

He sat on the step until the cigarette went out in his fingers, looking north and east, toward the
water, at nothing he could see.
