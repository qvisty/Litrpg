# Chapter Four

The errand was nothing. That was the part Margit would carry longest afterward, that the errand
was nothing at all.

A wireless valve, wrapped in a sock, in a coat pocket. Holger's set had gone dumb two nights past
with England waiting on it, and the replacement was sitting in a lawyer's desk drawer across town,
and somebody had to walk it over before the evening schedule. Not guns. Not explosive. Not eight
lives on a dark beach. A piece of glass the size of a thumb, and a grey afternoon with the first
real cold of the winter in it, and Aksel took the errand because he was the one standing there when
it needed taking.

"Back before the bell," he told Margit at the door, and she said something he did not quite catch
about the coffee that was not coffee, and that was the whole of their parting, because you do not
say goodbye over a sock.

The city was quiet in the way it was always quiet now, a held-breath quiet, everyone's eyes at the
middle distance. He crossed the canal by the fish market, where a man in a good coat was feeding
gulls off the rail, tossing crumbs up into the wind and watching them taken, and Aksel went past
him without a glance to spare, because the afternoon light was going and the schedule would not
wait.

The letters rose as he walked, unasked, warm in the way they had been warm all that week, like a
stove left burning in a room he had not entered yet.

> REFINED.
> Standing among the assessed: first.
> Ready.

First. He had stopped arguing with them about words like that. Somewhere out there were the others
whose standing he had been measured against, the other ledgers in other coats, and he had decided
it was like being told you had the best teeth in a prison, a fact, no use to anyone. Ready he did
not understand at all, and there was no one to ask, and the lawyer's street was two corners away,
so he filed it with all the other words the thing would not explain, and afterward, in the place
where afterward happens, no one would ever know that the last thing Aksel Holt filed away unread
was the only warning he ever got.

The control was on the second corner, and it was new, and it was nothing.

A lorry, a rope, six men checking papers under a swinging lamp because somebody had cut a cable in
the harbour that morning and somebody else had to be seen doing something about it. The men were
tired. The sergeant had his collar up against the cold. Aksel saw it all half a street away, read
it the way he read everything, bored men, a formality, papers good enough for better men than
these, and the weather behind his eyes said nothing, nothing at all, and he walked toward the lamp
with his gait gone heavy and tired, a clerk going home, a man with a sock in his pocket.

The world went provisional.

He had no other word for it. Later there would be no later to find one in. The street, the lamp,
the rope, the six tired men, all of it went thick and poised and held, the way the air holds
between the lightning and the sound, and in the middle of the held moment the letters came up one
last time, and they were not warm.

> THE THROW.

The boy at the rope was nineteen and had been on the coast road all night and his friend had been
shot at from a rooftop in the spring, in another town, and none of that was Aksel's and all of it
was in the boy's hands as they took the papers. A tram came round the far corner then and its
flange caught the curve of the rail the way tram flanges did a hundred times a day in that city,
a shriek of steel, an utterly ordinary sound, and the boy was tired and nineteen and already
half-turned toward it, and his rifle was already up because his sergeant liked rifles up, and
there is a distance a startled hand travels that has nothing in it that could be called a
decision. No one shouted. Nothing was discovered. The sock was never even searched.

> Against him.
> Withheld.

Aksel sat down against the wall of a shop that sold buttons. He had been shot once before, in the
shoulder, the year the war began for him, and it had felt like being hit with a bar, and this did
not feel like that at all. This felt like the tide going out. He put his hand flat against his own
chest the way he had once put it flat on a false floor with an old man breathing under it, and he
thought, with a great mild astonishment, that his hands were not shaking, and that Margit was going
to stand at the door tonight with the coffee that was not coffee, and he began to count, one
thousand and one, one thousand and two, because counting was how you kept the fear out of your
hands, and somewhere between the numbers the letters rose one final time, patient, exact, and
almost, if such a thing could be said of frost, tender.

> Kept faith under question.
> Assessed, in full.
> COMPLETION.

The last thing he saw was the gulls over the canal, turning up into the grey, being taken by the
wind one by one, and the last thing he did, the very last, was to reach for the letters that were
already fading, in front of six Germans and a swinging lamp, and try, with the whole of what
remained of him, to put himself between them and her name.

The ledger has no entry for that. It was, by every account it kept, worth nothing.

It was the most human thing he ever did.

<p style="text-align:center">* * *</p>

Across the city, on the back step of a stolen dance hall, Jonas Keller came up off the cold stone
like a man stung, with his hand at his own chest and his heart going, and stood in the dark not
knowing what he was listening for.

The warmth to the north and east had gone out.

Not faded. Gone out, between one breath and the next, the way a candle goes when a door opens
somewhere in the house, and in the space where it had been there was, for one terrible clear
instant, something else. Not words. He would spend years failing to describe it to himself. It was
the feeling of standing in a great cold room in which, very quietly and with perfect satisfaction,
someone had just been paid.

His own letters rose then, soft as breath on a window.

> Withdrawn from the count: one.
> Standing among the assessed: you have advanced.

"No," Jonas said, out loud, alone, to the wet dark over the roofs of a city that hated him
politely. He did not know what he was refusing. He only knew that somewhere in that city a stranger
he had been forbidden to look for was a warmth that had gone out, and that the cold thing behind
his eyes had counted it, and moved him up a rung, and expected him, in some wordless bookkept way,
to be pleased.

He sat back down on the step. After a while he noticed he was not weeping, and could not, and that
was the first time Jonas Keller was ever truly afraid of it, not of the war, not of the black
uniforms, but of the ledger and the tide it was taking him out on, one saved life, one kept score,
one dry-eyed midnight at a time.
